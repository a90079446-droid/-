# Natural dialogue: weak vs strong

Each pair shows the same dramatic content written two ways. The notes explain the difference. These are illustrations, not lines to reuse.

## 1. English (US): a hidden loan

**Weak**
```
SARAH: David, I found out that you took out a loan against our house without telling me. I feel so betrayed.
DAVID: Sarah, I'm sorry. I did it because the business was failing and I didn't want you to worry.
SARAH: How could you do this to me? We promised to share everything!
```

**Strong**
```
Sarah sits at the kitchen island. A bank envelope, already opened, under her hand.

SARAH: The bank called. They wanted to confirm your new number.
DAVID: Okay.
SARAH: Your new number, David.
DAVID: It's a work thing--
SARAH: Two hundred thousand is a work thing?

He reaches for the envelope. She doesn't move her hand.

DAVID: I was going to fix it before you--
SARAH: Before I what? [pause] Found out?
```

**Why it works**
- The weak version names emotions ("betrayed") and explains motives the audience can infer.
- In the strong version, information arrives as an attack (the "new number" detail), and the envelope carries the conflict visually.
- The name is used once, as a weapon. Interruptions show who is in control.
- Sarah finishes his sentence for him, which is crueller than any accusation.

## 2. English (UK): a sister comes home

**Weak**
```
EMMA: Hello, Kate. It has been five years since you left. Mum has been very ill and you never visited her.
KATE: I know, Emma. I feel terrible about it. I had my reasons.
```

**Strong**
```
EMMA: You found it, then.
KATE: Hasn't moved.
EMMA: No. Some of us stayed put.

Kate puts her bag down. Emma looks at it like it's a dog that might stay.

KATE: How is she?
EMMA: Ask her yourself. Second door. You'll remember.
KATE: Em--
EMMA: Kettle's just boiled. If you're stopping.
```

**Why it works**
- British understatement and indirectness: "If you're stopping" means *are you staying this time*.
- Five years of absence is in "Some of us stayed put" and "You'll remember", not in a stated number.
- The kettle line is hospitality and hostility at once.

## 3. Indonesian

### 3a. Jakarta friends (gue–lo)

**Weak (calqued, too formal for the relationship)**
```
RANI: Apakah kamu baik-baik saja? Kamu terlihat sangat sedih hari ini.
DINA: Aku baik-baik saja. Aku hanya merasa lelah karena pekerjaanku.
```

**Strong**
```
RANI: Lo kenapa sih? Dari tadi diem aja.
DINA: Nggak papa.
RANI: Dih. "Nggak papa"-nya kayak gitu.
DINA: [pause] Raka balik ke Jakarta.
RANI: ...Terus?
DINA: Terus dia ngajak ketemu. Besok.
```

**Why it works**
- "Apakah kamu baik-baik saja?" is a word-for-word "Are you okay?"; Jakarta friends say "Lo kenapa sih?"
- Particles ("sih", "dih") do real work and are not stacked on every line.
- Rani quoting "Nggak papa" back is how friends call a lie.
- The real topic (Raka) arrives late, after deflection.

### 3b. A Javanese family in Yogyakarta (no Jakarta slang)

```
NAYLA: Bu, Bapak udah minum obatnya?
IBU: Udah, Nduk. Kamu ndak usah mikir yang aneh-aneh.
NAYLA: Aku cuma nanya.
IBU: Iya. Nanyanya tiap jam.

Ibu keeps folding laundry. One of Bapak's shirts, she folds twice.
```

**Why it works**
- "Nduk" (to a daughter) and "ndak" mark a Javanese family; "gue/lo" would be wrong here.
- Nayla uses "aku" with her mother, "Bu" as address instead of "kamu".
- The mother's worry is in the shirt she folds twice, not in her words.

### 3c. A pronoun shift as a dramatic event
A couple who always use "aku–kamu":
```
RAKA: Kamu dari mana?
DINA: Saya rasa itu bukan urusan kamu lagi.
```
"Saya" from a partner who always said "aku" is a door closing. Use the shift once; it loses force if repeated.

## 4. Mandarin

### 4a. Mainland: mother and adult son

**Weak (translation-ese, over-explicit)**
```
妈妈：儿子，我非常担心你的未来。你已经三十岁了，你应该结婚了。
小宇：妈妈，我理解你的担心，但是我有我自己的想法。
```

**Strong**
```
妈：吃了没？
小宇：吃了。
妈：你二姨说，她单位新来了个姑娘，人挺好的。
小宇：妈。
妈：我就说说嘛。又没让你怎么样。
小宇：[pause] 我下个月调去成都。
妈：……那边冬天冷不冷啊？
```

**Why it works**
- "吃了没？" opens like a real call home. Pronouns are dropped.
- The marriage pressure arrives sideways through "二姨" and a colleague.
- "嘛" carries the mother's mock-innocence.
- Her reply to the move is about the weather: she can't say "don't go".

### 4b. Taiwan friends (Traditional characters)
```
小婷：欸你今天怎麼那麼早？
家豪：沒啊，就睡不著。
小婷：喔～又是她齁。
家豪：你很煩耶。
```
"欸", "喔", "齁", "耶" place this in Taiwan; Traditional characters, and 「」 quotes if any are needed.

## 5. Korean: a speech-level shift as a weapon

A married couple who normally speak 반말 to each other.

**Weak (stiff, wrong register, "당신" as a generic you)**
```
지은: 당신은 나에게 거짓말을 했습니다. 나는 당신에게 매우 실망했습니다.
민호: 미안합니다. 나는 당신을 걱정시키고 싶지 않았습니다.
```

**Strong**
```
민호: 왜 이렇게 늦었어? 밥은?
지은: 먹었어요.
민호: ……먹었어"요"?
지은: 네. 회사 앞에서 먹었어요. 신경 쓰지 마세요.

He puts his chopsticks down.

민호: 지은아. 왜 그래.
지은: 오늘 은행에서 전화 왔었어요.
```

**Why it works**
- Jieun switches from 반말 to 해요체 with her own husband. It is cold, deliberate distance, and he hears it instantly.
- Minho repeating "요" back makes the shift explicit without anyone explaining it.
- "지은아" is intimate; he's trying to pull her back into their usual register.
- The bank call lands as a fact, not an accusation.

## 6. Japanese: an after-hours confession that isn't one

**Weak**
```
佐藤：私はあなたのことが好きです。あなたは私のことをどう思っていますか？
```

**Strong**
```
佐藤：先輩、まだ残ってたんですか。
高木：うん。終わんなくてさ。

Sato puts a can of coffee on Takagi's desk.

佐藤：……これ、よかったら。
高木：え、いいの？
佐藤：買いすぎたんで。
高木：二本も？
佐藤：……二本も、です。
```

**Why it works**
- Sato stays in polite form with a senior; Takagi uses casual speech ("終わんなくてさ"). The hierarchy is in the grammar.
- "あなた" in direct address is almost always wrong in this kind of scene.
- The confession is the second can of coffee. "二本も、です" admits it without saying it.

## 7. Spanish

### 7a. Mexico City siblings
```
LUCÍA: ¿Ya le dijiste a mi mamá?
BETO: Ahorita le digo.
LUCÍA: "Ahorita." Llevas tres semanas con tu "ahorita".
BETO: Pues no es tan fácil, ¿eh?
```
"Ahorita" in Mexican Spanish can mean *right now* or *someday*; Lucía weaponizes the ambiguity. "Mi mamá" between siblings is natural in Mexico.

### 7b. Madrid friends
```
MARTA: Tía, ¿qué te pasa?
ELENA: Nada.
MARTA: Ya. Nada. Por eso llevas media hora mirando el móvil.
```
"Tía", "ya", and "móvil" place this in Spain. In Mexico it would be "celular", and "tía" would read as *aunt*.

## 8. Three characters at one table

Mother (HELEN), daughter (CLAIRE), Claire's fiancé (MARCUS). Helen wants to know why the wedding moved. Claire wants to stop her finding out. Marcus doesn't know there's anything to find out.

**Weak (round-robin, everyone explains)**
```
HELEN: Claire, why did you move the wedding to March?
CLAIRE: Mom, we moved it because Marcus's work schedule changed.
MARCUS: Yes, Mrs. Hall, my company is sending me to Singapore in April.
HELEN: I see. I'm just worried because it seems rushed.
CLAIRE: There's nothing to worry about, Mom.
```

**Strong**
```
HELEN: March. That's soon.
MARCUS: Blame Singapore. They want me there by April.
HELEN: Singapore. [to Claire] You didn't mention Singapore.
CLAIRE: I mentioned it.
HELEN: You said "a work trip."
MARCUS: It's a two-year posting, so--

Claire's knee knocks the table. The water glasses jump.

CLAIRE: Sorry.
HELEN: Two years. [to Marcus] And Claire's going with you.
MARCUS: [laughs] Well, that's the idea--

He stops. Claire is looking at her plate.
```

**Why it works**
- Each character has a different stake; the lines come from those stakes.
- Not round-robin: Helen and Marcus talk *past* Claire, which is the trap.
- `[to Claire]` / `[to Marcus]` mark who is being addressed, which matters for staging and voice work.
- Claire's knee hitting the table is the reveal: Marcus doesn't know what Helen just guessed.
