## 1. The format and its viewers

Vertical short drama (TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, dedicated short-drama apps) is watched on phones, often with sound off at first, often while doing something else, with a thumb ready to scroll. Writing implications:
- The first image must be readable at a glance, in a 9:16 frame.
- The core conflict should be understandable even with sound off for the first seconds; key visual information must be on screen.
- Viewers often arrive at a random episode. Each episode needs enough orientation to follow without a recap, delivered through action, not a narrator.
- Series often run dozens of episodes. The engine must be repeatable and escalating.

## 2. Durations and what each can hold

| Length | Typical scope | Speech budget (approx., English at drama pace) |
|---|---|---|
| 15–30 s micro | One moment, one turn | 20–60 words |
| 30–60 s | One scene, one escalation, one turn | 50–120 words |
| 60–90 s (default) | One to three scenes; hook, escalation, turn, ending beat | 100–200 words |
| 2–3 min | Two to five scenes; a mini-arc; a secondary thread | 200–400 words |
| Longer | Full scene structure | Budget per scene |

Speech budgets drop when the episode depends on silence, reactions, and action, and they vary by language. Use `runtime-timing.md` for the method.

## 3. Episode shape

Every episode should have:
- **Opening hook** (first 1–3 s): an image and/or line that poses a question.
- **Main dramatic objective**: what the protagonist is trying to do *in this episode*.
- **Conflict progression**: at least one escalation.
- **Meaningful interaction**: characters acting on each other, not just reporting.
- **Development**: by the end, something is different (knowledge, relationship, position, emotion).
- **Ending beat**: a consequence, decision, threat, reveal, or emotional landing.

Not every episode resolves its conflict. Most series episodes should resolve the *episode's* question while advancing the *series'* question.

## 4. Retention: why viewers stay

Every beat must do at least one job:
- Advance the plot.
- Develop a character.
- Increase tension.
- Reveal information.
- Create an emotional reaction.
- Establish a visual detail that pays off later.
- Deliver a payoff.

**Retention techniques that are storytelling, not tricks**
- **Open loops:** raise a question early, answer it late in the episode (or in the next).
- **Dramatic irony:** let the viewer know something a character doesn't.
- **Escalating micro-turns:** something small shifts every 10–20 seconds (a new fact, a new arrival, a changed tactic).
- **Promised confrontation:** show both sides preparing; the viewer stays to see them meet.
- **Clear wants:** the viewer always knows what the protagonist is trying to do right now.

**What loses viewers**
- Setup without conflict (introductions, walking, greeting).
- Repetition of known information.
- Confusion about who is who or where we are.
- A hook that the episode abandons.

**Overlapping loops.** Open the next question before you close the current one. If the episode's first question is answered at second 40 and nothing new is open, the viewer is free to leave at second 41. Hand off: the answer itself raises the next problem ("Yes, he signed it. On the day she was in hospital.").

**Re-hooks.** Viewers drop off at predictable points: after the hook pays out, in the middle, and just before the end. Put a small jolt at each one: a new fact, an arrival, a changed tactic, a line that lands harder than expected. In a 60–90 s episode that usually means something sharp around 25–35 s and again around 55–65 s. These are not extra twists; they are the escalation beats the episode already has, placed where attention dips.

**Partial payoffs.** Give the viewer some of what they want in every episode, never all of it. The villain is embarrassed but not exposed; the couple almost touch hands; the heroine finds one page of the contract. Satisfaction keeps viewers trusting the series; withholding the full payoff keeps them watching it.

**Cut on the inhale.** End at the moment of highest tension, just before the answer: the door opening, the phone ringing a second time, the character drawing breath to speak. Cutting after the reaction gives the viewer closure and permission to stop. Cutting before it makes the next episode the only relief. This is different from a fake cliffhanger: the next episode must pick up from that exact moment and pay it honestly.

## 5. Pacing without losing feeling

Fast is not the same as good. The audience needs time to:
- **Understand** a reveal: a held reaction, 1–2 seconds.
- **Process** a turn: one beat of silence or a cut to the person most affected.
- **Feel** the cost: a quiet image after the climax.

Cut repetition, not breath. A 75-second episode with one 2-second silent beat at the right place usually lands harder than a 75-second episode filled wall to wall with dialogue.

Pacing tools:
- **Compression:** start late, leave early, skip transitions with hard cuts.
- **Contrast:** a quiet beat after a loud one; a still shot after movement.
- **Rhythm of lines:** short-short-long; a long speech broken by a one-word answer.

## 6. Vertical framing as a writing concern

- Favour **one or two people in frame**. Three-person confrontations work through cutting between singles and pairs, and through clear positions.
- **Faces and hands** read well; wide landscapes and crowded rooms read poorly.
- **Vertical staging:** stairs, doorways, people standing over seated people, phones held up, a figure at the top of a staircase. Height differences show status naturally.
- **Text on screen** (messages, documents) must be short and large enough to read.
- Write the action lines so a director can stage them in 9:16 without reinventing the geography.

## 7. Cliffhanger and ending variety

Rotate ending types across a series (see `plot-structure.md` §7): revelation sting, decision, threat, emotional landing, reversal of victory, a reframing line, an arrival.

Track the last few ending types in the series bible. If the last two episodes ended with "someone appears in the doorway", the next should end differently.

Cliffhangers must:
- Grow from this episode's conflict.
- Be dealt with in the next episode (not ignored or explained away in a line).
- Be honest: no "it was a dream", no misleading edit that the next episode reverses.

## 8. Episode writing deliverables (Workflow C)

When writing an episode, provide (briefly, then the script):
1. **Episode objective**: the episode's dramatic question and what changes by the end. For a series episode, also fill the short episode brief in `hooks-and-cliffhangers.md` §11 (cold-viewer line, double-duty detail, final image, next-episode question).
2. **Opening hook**: the first image and line, and the viewer's want it feeds (see `storytelling.md` §5).
3. **Beat progression**: a numbered list of 4–8 beats with approximate timecodes. Mark the re-hook beats and the beat where the next question opens.
4. **Screenplay** in the template format, with dialogue.
5. **Ending beat**: name its type.
6. **Estimated runtime** (if a runtime was specified, or the user cares).
7. **Continuity notes / state of story after the episode** when it's part of a series.

Skip any element the user said they don't want.

## 9. Common short-form failures

- Every episode opens with a slap, a reveal on a phone, or a public humiliation.
- The protagonist is humiliated for 20 episodes with no progress; frustration without movement.
- Villains are cartoonish so the payoff is cheap.
- Cliffhangers resolved by "it wasn't what it looked like".
- Episodes so dense with dialogue they cannot fit their runtime.
- Plot logic sacrificed for shock (the twist contradicts episode 3).
- No breathing room: every second is a scream.
- The episode answers its question and then keeps going, with nothing new open.
- The ending holds on the reaction until the tension is gone, so the viewer has no reason to tap "next".
- Curiosity only: the viewer wonders what happens but doesn't care who it happens to.
- A story that works on the page but not on screen: the context is in dialogue, the opening is setup, the middle repeats, and the ending pauses instead of changing something. See `hooks-and-cliffhangers.md`.
