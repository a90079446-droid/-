## 1. The building blocks

| Block | What it does | Short-form note |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Establishes who, where, what is normal, what is wanted | Compress to an image or one line; fold it into the hook |
| Inciting incident | Breaks the normal; forces a want into action | Often *is* the hook in 60-second episodes |
| Rising action | Attempts, obstacles, complications | One or two moves per episode, not five |
| Escalation | Each obstacle costs more or narrows the options | Raise stakes, reduce time, add a witness, remove an ally |
| Reversal | The situation flips: winner becomes loser, ally becomes threat | The engine of most good episodes |
| Reveal | New information changes the meaning of what we've seen | Must be visual or spoken by someone with a reason to say it |
| Climax | The decisive confrontation or choice | In a series, the episode's climax may be small |
| Resolution | The new normal and its cost | Often a single image; may be skipped for a cliffhanger |
| Cliffhanger / ending beat | Leaves a question, emotion, or threat | Must arise from this episode's conflict |

These are tools. Use the ones the story needs. A quiet 30-second romance piece may have only setup, one turn, and an image.

## 2. Structures by runtime

**15–30 seconds (micro).** One situation, one turn.
- Image that raises a question (0–3 s) → a line or action that sharpens it → the turn (the reveal, the reply, the choice) → a final reaction held long enough to register.

**30–60 seconds.**
- Hook (0–3 s) → orientation inside the conflict (to ~10 s) → one escalation → a turn or reveal (~35–45 s) → an ending beat.

**60–90 seconds (default vertical episode).**
- Hook (0–3 s): image plus the first problem.
- Pressure (3–25 s): the want meets resistance; positions become clear.
- Escalation (25–55 s): a new fact, a new arrival, or a move by the opponent raises the cost.
- Turn (55–75 s): reversal or reveal; the scene changes direction.
- Ending beat (75–90 s): consequence, decision, or a hook into the next episode.

**2–3 minutes.**
- Room for two or three scenes, a short B-thread (a secondary character's want), and a breath after the turn. Use a mini three-act shape: problem, complication with a midpoint shift, a climax and its consequence.

**Longer scenes / pilots.** Use full three-act or sequence structure (eight sequences of roughly ten minutes for a feature, fewer for a pilot), but keep the short-form instinct: enter late, leave early.

## 3. Escalation

Escalation is not "louder". It is a narrowing of options or a rise in cost. Ways to escalate:

- **Raise the price:** what is lost now includes something new (a child, a job, a secret).
- **Shorten the clock:** the meeting moves up; the flight leaves tonight.
- **Add a witness:** the private conflict becomes public.
- **Remove a resource:** the ally walks away; the evidence is destroyed.
- **Make it personal:** the antagonist turns out to know exactly where to hurt.
- **Force a choice:** two goods that cannot both be had, or two evils, one of which must be chosen.

Each episode of a series should leave the protagonist in a position that is, in some way, worse, more complicated, or more committed than before, even when they win the scene.

## 4. Reversals

A reversal flips the scene's direction. The strongest are prepared by what the audience already saw.

- **Power reversal:** the person begging becomes the person with leverage ("Before you call security, you should know who signed your loan.").
- **Knowledge reversal:** the person who thought they knew discovers they didn't, or vice versa.
- **Moral reversal:** the person we judged turns out to have had a reason; the person we trusted turns out to have a motive.
- **Goal reversal:** what the character wanted turns out to be the thing that will destroy them.

Do not reverse every scene. A string of reversals with no consequences becomes noise. After a reversal, let the characters react to it.

## 5. Reveals and twists

**A twist must:**
1. Surprise: the audience did not expect it in this form or at this moment.
2. Make sense in hindsight: replaying the story, it fits.
3. Be planted: at least one earlier, visible clue supports it (an object, a line, an odd reaction).
4. Change understanding: it alters what earlier scenes meant, not just what happens next.
5. Have consequences: characters must act on it.

**Fair vs unfair**

| Unfair (avoid) | Fair |
|---|---|
| The killer is a character we never saw | The killer is the helpful neighbour we saw three times, always near the stairwell |
| The reveal relies on a fact the camera deliberately hid while showing us everything else | The camera showed the fact; we read it wrongly because of what someone said |
| A character acts against their established nature, unmotivated, to create the twist | The twist reinterprets their behaviour so that it was consistent all along |
| "It was all a dream" | The dream was a plant; the reality is worse |
| The twist contradicts something established | The twist recontextualizes something established |

**Plant–clue–payoff.** For each planned twist, write down: the plant (where it first appears), at least one reinforcing clue, the misdirection (why the audience reads it wrongly), and the payoff (when and how it lands). Keep this in the series bible.

**Twist frequency.** A short-drama series needs reveals, but not a shocking twist every episode. Alternate: a reveal, an escalation, an emotional confrontation, a quiet episode with a sting at the end. Unearned shocks wear out the audience.

**Distinguish a reveal from an inconsistency.** If a reader would say "but that contradicts episode 3", it is a mistake, not a twist. Either rewrite the earlier material (if the user allows) or find a version that fits.

## 6. Setups and payoffs

- Anything given emphasis (a close-up, a repeated line, a lingering object) is a promise. Pay it off or remove the emphasis.
- The best payoffs use the setup in a new way: the bracelet she gave him as a gift is the evidence that convicts him.
- In long series, keep a list of open setups (series bible: "planted, not yet paid off").
- Pay off in proportion: a heavily emphasized setup needs a substantial payoff.

## 7. Endings and cliffhangers

**Ending beat types** (vary them):
- **Revelation sting:** a new fact in the last seconds.
- **Decision:** a character commits to something irreversible ("Book the flight.").
- **Threat:** we see what is coming that the character doesn't.
- **Emotional landing:** no plot sting, just a held image that makes the cost felt.
- **Reversal of the episode's victory:** the win turns out to be a trap.
- **Question in a character's mouth:** a line that reframes the episode ("Then whose child did you bury?").
- **Arrival:** someone appears who changes the board.

**Cliffhanger rules**
- It grows from the episode's conflict, not from nowhere.
- It must be answerable: the next episode deals with it rather than ignoring it.
- No fake cliffhangers (the gunshot that turns out to be a car backfiring, every time).
- Rotate types. Five "a stranger appears in the doorway" endings in a row is a pattern the audience will see.

**The payoff ladder (series).** A binge series is a promise paid in instalments. List the viewer's big wants (see `storytelling.md` §5), then plan how each one is paid across the series:

| Want | Small payoff | Bigger payoff | Full payoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| See the husband exposed | Ep 4: his mother notices the second phone | Ep 11: the wife finds the apartment lease | Ep 22: exposed at his own promotion dinner |

- Pay something every episode, even if it is small: a win, a look, a door closing on the villain.
- Before a full payoff, raise the price of it: the closer the exposure, the more the protagonist has to lose by making it.
- After a full payoff, open a bigger want straight away (the exposure reveals someone worse). A series with no unpaid want left ends there, whatever the episode count says.
- On short-drama apps with a paywall, the episode just before it should end on the series' strongest anticipation so far, built from what came before, not a stunt invented for the moment.

**Resolution.** A series ending must answer the central question, show the cost, and show who the characters are now. Avoid the generic inspirational close ("and she learned that true strength comes from within"). Prefer a concrete image that proves the change.

## 8. Story development deliverables (Workflow B)

Given an approved concept, deliver:

1. **Synopsis**: half a page to a page, beginning to end, including the ending.
2. **Character profiles** for leads and key supporting roles (`templates/character-profile.md`).
3. **Structure**: the arc of the series in acts or phases, with the midpoint and the turn into the final phase.
4. **Major plot points**: inciting incident, first commitment, midpoint reversal, darkest point, climax, resolution.
5. **Reveals plan**: each twist with plant, clues, misdirection, payoff episode.
6. **Ending direction**: how it ends, what it costs, what image closes it.
7. **Payoff ladder**: the viewer's two or three big wants and the episodes where each is paid in part and in full (§7).
8. **Episode breakdown**: one or two lines per episode stating the episode question, the key turn, the payoff given, and the ending beat type (to keep variety visible).
9. **Series bible** (`templates/series-bible.md`) if the project will run many episodes.

## 9. Structural failure patterns

- **Flat middle:** episodes repeat the same conflict at the same level. Fix: escalate per section 3.
- **Idiot plot:** the story only works because characters ignore the obvious. Fix: give them a reason not to act (fear, cost, misinformation planted by someone with a motive).
- **Coincidence carrying weight:** coincidence may start trouble, never solve it.
- **Exposition dump:** information delivered as a speech. Fix: make the information contested, overheard, discovered, or forced out.
- **Escalation without consequence:** big events that nobody reacts to in the next episode.
- **Twist inflation:** each reveal bigger and less believable than the last.
- **The protagonist is rescued:** someone else solves the climax. Let the protagonist make the decisive move.
