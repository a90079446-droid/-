## 1. What a premise must contain

A working premise answers six questions in one or two sentences:

| Question | Weak answer | Working answer |
|---|---|---|
| Who? | "A young woman" | "A night-shift pharmacist who has never once made a mistake" |
| What is interesting about the situation? | "She finds out a secret" | "She notices her own handwriting on a prescription she never wrote" |
| What does she want? | "The truth" | "To find out who forged it before the audit on Friday" |
| What blocks her? | "Obstacles" | "The only person with access is her younger brother, whom she got the job" |
| What is at stake? | "Everything" | "Her licence, and the brother she raised" |
| Why should we care? | "It's emotional" | "Protecting him and protecting herself have become the same choice, and she can't make both" |

If you cannot fill all six with specifics, the premise is not ready. Ask, or propose specific options.

A premise should be explainable in one breath to someone who has never heard of the project. If explaining it needs backstory, a list of rules, or "it's complicated", simplify the engine, not the telling.

## 2. The dramatic mechanism

The mechanism is the specific arrangement that forces conflict, the thing that makes this story unlike every other story in its genre. Useful mechanisms:

- **Forced proximity with opposed goals.** Two people who cannot leave each other but want incompatible things (the bodyguard hired to protect the witness who will testify against his father).
- **Dramatic irony.** The audience knows what a character does not, and watches them walk toward it (we know the "new nanny" is the birth mother; the adoptive mother does not).
- **A ticking constraint.** A deadline, a countdown, a limited resource (the transfer clears at midnight; the wedding is Saturday).
- **A secret with a carrier.** Someone holds information that would change everything, and has reasons not to tell.
- **A rule with a cost.** A world rule that creates dilemmas (in this family, whoever inherits the restaurant must cook the founder's dish blind; she has lost her sense of taste).
- **Identity pressure.** A character living as someone they are not, where exposure carries a price.
- **Inverted status.** The humiliated becomes powerful, or the powerful becomes dependent, and both must keep acting as before.

One strong mechanism is better than three weak ones. Short-form series often run on one mechanism for many episodes, escalating it rather than replacing it.

## 3. Stakes the audience feels

Stakes are what is lost if the protagonist fails. Make them:

- **Personal before global.** "Her daughter will stop speaking to her" lands harder in 60 seconds than "the company will collapse".
- **Visible.** Put the stake on screen: the eviction notice on the door, the child's empty chair, the ring she stops wearing.
- **Escalating.** Each episode should either raise the cost of failure or narrow the protagonist's options.
- **Two-sided.** The best stakes have a cost on both sides: telling the truth loses the marriage; hiding it loses her self-respect.

Avoid stakes that are only stated ("If this fails, I lose everything!"). If the audience must be told what is at stake, it isn't on screen yet.

## 4. The central dramatic question

Every story, and every episode, has a yes/no question the audience is waiting to see answered. "Will she expose him before the wedding?" "Will he admit the child is his?" Write it down before writing scenes.

- The series question can stay open for many episodes. Each episode needs its own smaller question that it answers, while opening or sharpening the next.
- If an episode has no question, it is a summary, not drama.
- If the question is answered in the first ten seconds, the rest of the episode must ask a new one.

## 5. Hooks for short-form drama

On vertical platforms the viewer decides in about two seconds. The hook is the first image and the first line, together.

**Hook techniques** (choose for the story, not by rotation):

| Technique | Example opening | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Start mid-consequence | A man is signing divorce papers; his hand stops at a clause he didn't expect. | Needs fast orientation afterward |
| Unexpected revelation | A bride's phone lights up: the photo is of her groom, asleep, in a hotel bed, sent from her sister's number. | Overused if it's always a phone |
| Unusual visual event | A funeral where the widow is the only one smiling. | Must be explained by the end |
| Public humiliation | A waitress is ordered to kneel and clean a CEO's shoe in front of a full restaurant. | Becomes formula fast; needs a fresh angle |
| Conflicting intentions | Two sisters smile for a family photo; under the table, one is texting the police. | Needs clear visual geography |
| Dramatic irony | We see the poison go into the cup; the victim thanks the poisoner for the tea. | Tone must hold |
| Suspicious behaviour | A husband washes a single wine glass at 3 a.m. and puts it back in the wrong cupboard. | Subtle; needs a clear close-up |
| Impossible situation | A woman receives a voicemail from her own phone number, in her own voice. | Must have a real answer |
| Emotional confrontation already burning | "You told her before you told me?" as the first line, no setup. | Needs a payoff of equal heat |
| Immediate stakes | A mother has eleven minutes to get her son out of a building she is not allowed to enter. | Must deliver on the clock |

**Hook rules**
- The hook must belong to this story. It sets up the episode's question, not a different, more exciting story.
- Do not fake the hook. A dream, a misleading edit, or a "just kidding" reveal betrays the viewer and costs trust across the series.
- Start late. Cut the walk to the door, the greeting, the small talk. The episode begins where the trouble already exists.
- Image first, words second. A strong opening often has no dialogue for the first second or two.
- Vary hooks across a series. Five episodes that open with a slap teach the audience to scroll.

### Three engines: curiosity, anticipation, desire

A hook that only raises a question makes the viewer curious. Curiosity keeps a viewer for one episode. What keeps them for twenty is wanting something to happen. Know which engine each hook runs on, and stack two when you can.

| Engine | What the viewer feels | Built from | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Curiosity | "What is that? Who is it?" | A gap in information | A text from an unknown number in a dead man's coat |
| Anticipation | "Something is coming, and I know it" | Dramatic irony, a visible clock, a planted weapon | We saw the forged signature; the heir is about to read the will aloud |
| Desire | "I need to see this happen" | A wrong the viewer witnessed, a feeling held back, a person who deserves to find out | The sister who stole the fiancé is giving a toast; the bride is holding the hotel receipt and smiling |

- **Desire is the strongest binge engine.** It is built by showing a wrong (or a love, or a secret) clearly and early, then making the viewer wait for the moment it comes out. The viewer stays to see the face of the person who finds out.
- **Stack engines.** Curiosity plus desire: we don't know who "M" is, but we already want the widow to stop protecting the husband who lied to her.
- **Name the viewer's want.** Before writing an episode, write one line: "By now the viewer wants ___." If you can't fill it, the episode is running on curiosity alone.

### Drafting and testing a hook

Don't write one hook and keep it. Draft three to five that use different techniques or engines, then test each:

1. **Scroll test.** With the first frame alone, is something wrong, about to happen, or out of place? A face, a hand, an object in close-up beats a wide establishing shot.
2. **Sound-off test.** Can the viewer read the conflict without audio? If the hook needs its line to make sense, add a visual that carries half of it.
3. **Specificity test.** Swap the characters for any other drama. If the hook still works unchanged, it is generic. Make it something only this story could open with.
4. **Promise test.** What does the hook promise, and does the episode pay it (or pay a part of it and raise the price of the rest)?
5. **Second-question test.** By second 10, has the hook led into a second question or want? A hook that answers itself and stops loses the viewer at second 11.

Keep the strongest, say why in one line, and save a good reject for a later episode or an ending.

## 6. Genre promises

Genre is a promise of a specific pleasure. Know which one you are delivering.

| Genre | The pleasure promised | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Revenge | Watching a wronged person outmanoeuvre the people who wronged them | Revenge without cost; the victim becomes as cruel as the villain without the story noticing |
| Romance | Two people overcoming what keeps them apart, and earned intimacy | Obstacles that a conversation would fix |
| Betrayal / family drama | Loyalty tested, secrets exposed, consequences that last | Characters acting stupidly to keep the secret alive |
| Mystery | Clues the audience can assemble; a fair, surprising answer | Answers that rely on information we never saw |
| Thriller | Danger escalating faster than the protagonist can respond | Random danger with no logic |
| Status reversal ("underdog rises") | The humiliated proving their worth | One-note villains; the rise requires no effort |
| Comedy-drama | Truth about people through absurd situations | Jokes that undercut every emotional beat |
| Supernatural / time-loop / rebirth | A rule-bound impossible situation explored for emotional truth | Rules that change whenever the plot needs |

Deliver the core pleasure in every episode, at least a little. Combine genres deliberately (revenge romance, workplace mystery) but know which one leads.

## 7. Developing concepts (Workflow A)

When asked for concepts, deliver for each:

- **Title** (working): a series title that passes the plain-sentence test; see `references/titles.md`. Add upload titles when the user wants them.
- **Logline**: one or two sentences, containing the mechanism.
- **Genre and tone**, plus the core pleasure.
- **Core conflict**: who wants what, against whom.
- **Main characters**: two to four, each with one line of want and one line of contradiction.
- **Central dramatic question.**
- **Opening hook**: the first image and line of episode 1, and the engine it runs on (curiosity, anticipation, desire).
- **The viewer's want**: what the audience will be aching to see by episode 3 (the confession, the exposure, the first kiss, the villain's face when the tables turn). If a concept has no answer here, it may fill a few episodes but not a binge.
- **Direction**: where the series goes and roughly how long it can sustain the engine (e.g. "20–30 episodes; midpoint reveal flips the alliance").
- **What makes it distinctive**, in one line.

Offer distinct options when asked for several: different mechanisms, not the same story in three costumes. Recommend one and say why. Keep the whole response scannable.

## 8. Premise red flags

- The protagonist is passive: things happen to them, they never choose.
- The antagonist has no reason beyond "evil" or "jealous".
- The conflict depends on someone not saying an obvious sentence.
- The stakes are abstract ("her future", "everything").
- The hook is a coincidence ("she happens to overhear...") with nothing earned behind it.
- The premise needs exposition to make sense.
- It is a genre label, not a situation ("a CEO romance").
- The ending is already obvious from the logline, with no question about how or at what cost.
