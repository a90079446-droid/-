## 1. What an important character needs

For leads and key supporting roles, establish (see `templates/character-profile.md`):

- **Identity:** name, age, occupation, where they live, social position.
- **Background:** the two or three past events that still drive them, not a biography.
- **Personality:** how they meet the world (guarded, charming, blunt, anxious, controlling).
- **Want (goal):** the concrete thing they pursue in this story.
- **Need:** what they actually lack, often in tension with the want.
- **Fear:** what they will do almost anything to avoid.
- **Desire:** the private hope they rarely admit.
- **Flaw:** the habit of mind that causes their problems.
- **Contradiction:** the trait that seems not to fit, and makes them human (a ruthless lawyer who cannot fire her incompetent assistant).
- **Relationships:** who matters to them and the history between them.
- **Behavioural patterns:** what they do under pressure (go silent, joke, attack, apologize, clean).
- **Emotional vulnerabilities:** the topic, person, or word that gets past their defences.
- **Speech pattern:** see section 3.

Minor characters need less: a function, one specific trait, and a way of speaking that is not the default.

## 2. Want, need, fear, flaw, contradiction

These five drive behaviour more than backstory does.

| Element | Example: Arini, 34, wedding-dress tailor |
|---|---|
| Want | Keep the business her late mother built |
| Need | To stop living for other people's approval |
| Fear | Being seen as the daughter who failed |
| Flaw | Says yes to everything, then resents it in silence |
| Contradiction | Gentle with clients, merciless with her own sister |

A scene works when a character's want collides with someone else's, and the flaw makes it harder. A character arc works when pursuing the want forces the character to face the need.

Behaviour comes from these elements under pressure. Arini, betrayed, would not scream in public; she would agree politely, finish the hem perfectly, and then do something precise and devastating.

## 3. Voice: making every character sound like themselves

Test: cover the speaker names. Can you still tell who is talking? If not, the voices need work.

**Dimensions of voice**

| Dimension | Range | Example contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary | plain ↔ technical ↔ ornate | "He's lying." / "His statement is inconsistent with the timeline." |
| Sentence length | clipped ↔ long and looping | "No." / "Look, I'm not saying no, I'm saying not today, not like this." |
| Directness | blunt ↔ evasive | "You cheated." / "So. Busy week?" |
| Humour | none ↔ dry ↔ constant | |
| Confidence | commands ↔ hedges | "Sit." / "Maybe we could, um, sit?" |
| Emotional restraint | says everything ↔ says nothing | |
| Habits | verbal tics, repeated phrases, how they open or close a line | Always answers a question with a question |
| Register | formal ↔ street | depends on class, age, region, situation |
| Rhythm | fast overlap ↔ slow and deliberate | |
| What they avoid saying | the name of the dead brother; the word "sorry" | |

Pick two or three strong markers per character and use them consistently, lightly. A verbal tic in every line becomes a parody.

**Voice is also silence.** One character fills every pause; another lets the other person squirm. That contrast is characterisation.

**Voice changes by situation, not by accident.** A character sounds different furious than relaxed, but it is still the same person: the clipped one gets more clipped; the talker gets more frantic.

## 4. Relationships shape speech and behaviour

The same person speaks differently to:

| Listener | What typically changes |
|---|---|
| Spouse / long-term partner | Shorthand, interruptions, old grievances in passing, private nicknames, less politeness |
| Stranger | More politeness, fuller sentences, guarded content |
| Parent | Childhood patterns return; deference or rebellion; topics avoided |
| Child | Simplified, protective, sometimes lying "for their own good" |
| Subordinate | Commands, less explanation, sometimes performed warmth |
| Superior | Hedging, formality, honorifics, strategic agreement |
| Romantic interest (early) | Testing, teasing, under-saying, over-saying |
| Enemy | Courtesy as a weapon, or naked contempt; precise word choice |
| Close friend | Teasing, swearing (if in character), honesty, shared references |

**Emotional history** must carry into every exchange. Siblings who fought over an inheritance do not chat neutrally at the next family dinner. Former lovers do not speak like colleagues, even when they are trying to.

In languages with grammatical politeness (Korean, Japanese, Javanese, and others) the relationship is encoded in grammar; a change of speech level is a dramatic event. See `multilingual-writing.md`.

## 5. Consistency and motivated change

- Keep a short "behaviour sheet" per character in the bible: what they would never do, what they always do under pressure.
- **Change must be caused.** A coward becomes brave because of something we watched happen, and the change costs something. If a twist needs a character to act out of character, find a motive that makes it in character, or change the twist.
- **Change is gradual.** One event cracks a habit; it does not erase it. A guarded character who finally confides still struggles with the words.
- **Knowledge consistency.** A character can only act on what they know. Track who knows what (see `episode-continuity.md`). A character who "suddenly knows" a secret needs an on-screen or clearly implied source.
- **Skill consistency.** If she couldn't drive in episode 2, she can't do a car chase in episode 9 without an explanation.

## 6. Antagonists and supporting roles

**Antagonists**
- Give them a goal they believe is justified. The mother-in-law who sabotages the marriage believes she is protecting her son; she may even be partly right.
- Give them competence. A weak antagonist makes the protagonist's victory worthless.
- Give them a line they won't cross, or a person they love. It makes them dangerous and human.
- In revenge stories, let the antagonist's crime be specific and shown, so the audience's appetite for justice is earned.

**Supporting characters**
- Every supporting character wants something in the scene, even if small (the waiter wants this table to leave so his shift can end).
- Avoid the "explainer" friend whose only job is to ask questions so the protagonist can deliver exposition.
- Use supporting characters to pressure the lead: the best friend who needs a favour at the worst moment.

**Ensembles (three or more in a scene).** Give each a different stake in the outcome, and let alliances shift during the scene. See `natural-dialogue.md` §5.

## 7. Character red flags

- Two characters could swap lines without anyone noticing.
- A character's only trait is their role ("the rich husband", "the evil sister").
- Motivation is stated in dialogue rather than shown in choices ("I'm doing this because I've always felt unloved").
- A character forgets an emotional event from the previous episode.
- Everyone is articulate, self-aware, and emotionally fluent.
- Everyone is either a saint or a monster.
- A character exists only to deliver information, then disappears.
