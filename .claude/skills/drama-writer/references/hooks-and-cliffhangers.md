## 1. Why a good story still fails on screen

A script can be well plotted and still lose a stranger in ten seconds. The usual causes:

- The opening is atmosphere or backstory, not a problem happening now.
- The context lives in dialogue or narration, so the picture alone says nothing.
- The reveal is the episode's point, but nothing earlier made the viewer want it.
- The middle repeats one note; nothing gets worse.
- The ending is a pause, not a change.

Story quality is necessary and not enough. The viewer is a stranger on a phone who has seen none of your notes.

## 2. The four things a hook must establish

In the first seconds, on screen:

1. **A character**: who is affected.
2. **A visible situation**: what is happening now.
3. **One thing that does not fit**: an abnormal detail, a conflict, a wrong.
4. **A question the viewer urgently wants answered.**

The hook does not explain history, world rules or the full goal. It makes the present change legible.

> A woman steps into an elevator holding an old hotel keycard. The display goes from 12 to 13. The building has no 13th floor.

Character, place, prop, anomaly, question: one actor, one location, one prop. Compare "Nia had always been curious about the hotel where her mother worked." That is background. Open on the elevator and reveal the mother when it changes the danger.

## 3. First-watch clarity: the cold viewer

Most viewers arrive at a random episode, sound off, with no knowledge of the series. Write for them. By roughly second 5 to 8 a stranger must be able to say:

- **Who** is the lead, and who is the opponent or the person they are facing?
- **What** is the relationship between them (marriage, boss and employee, debtor and creditor)?
- **Where does the power sit, and is it about to move?** Who looks smaller, who looks in charge, and is that wrong?
- **What is at stake right now?**

Deliver this with things the camera shows: uniform, car, job tool, ring, signature, logo, seating, who stands. Where a relationship or status cannot be shown fast enough, use a short caption or on-screen label (a line of text, a name card, a chat header) rather than a spoken explanation. Never open with a narrator reading the premise.

Dramatic irony is the fastest context. If the premise hides a truth from the characters (the "driver" actually owns the company), the viewer can be told it early, by a visual the characters miss, so every humiliation scene turns into anticipation. A twist hidden from the viewer too must still be preceded by a clear, wanted situation.

**Cold-viewer test.** Pretend you have only the first 8 seconds and no sound. Write one sentence for each: who, relation, wrong or danger, what they will probably do next. If you cannot, fix the opening before anything else.

## 4. Hook patterns

Pick the pattern for the story. Do not rotate by habit.

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Consequence before cause | Show the result, hold the decision | A bride walks into an empty hall with a marriage certificate already signed by both |
| Interrupted intention | Clear plan, then evidence makes it dangerous | A junior doctor is about to hand in her resignation when a new patient is admitted under her dead father's name |
| Impossible evidence | An inspectable object contradicts belief | A delivery driver is sent footage of himself entering an apartment he has never visited. His cracked watch and today's date are in frame |
| Public relationship shift | Trust, status or loyalty changes in front of others | At an engagement dinner, the fiancé texts: "Don't let my brother see your phone." The brother is across the table |
| Costly opportunity | The wanted thing carries a price | A musician is offered her dream contract, but must sign before hearing the final track |
| Broken rule | Show the rule, then the violation | Every door stays open during the nightly inspection. At midnight one sealed door knocks from inside |

Rules for any of them:
- The consequence, evidence or violation must be concrete and filmable. "Something strange appears on his phone" is empty. A timestamp, a watch, a signature, a name is evidence.
- The hook must be explained or reinterpreted by the episode. A striking image with no tie to the plot is a broken promise.
- Starting on a decision already in motion makes the lead active from frame one.

## 5. Turn the hook into visible actions

Before dialogue, list the actions that make the hook understandable with the sound off. For the elevator:

1. Nia grips an old keycard with the hotel logo.
2. The display moves from 12 to 13.
3. She sees the panel has no 13 button.
4. Her thumb hovers over the emergency control.
5. The doors open on an unfinished corridor.

Each action adds information, confirms the anomaly, or removes an easy exit.

**Silent-hook test.** Can a viewer identify the lead, see what changed, recognise the anomaly, form a specific question, and see what the lead can do right now? If it only works because a line explains it, move that information into an action, prop, screen, reaction or position.

## 6. What a cliffhanger must do

A cliffhanger changes the dramatic situation before the cut. It does not just withhold an answer. A strong ending has:

- a decision, discovery, threat or consequence;
- a link to something established earlier;
- a new problem the lead must now deal with;
- one specific question the next episode can start from.

End after the change is clear and before its consequence resolves. "Identity confirmed" at the keycard slot works because the card, the elevator and the missing floor were all set up. The next question is exact: whose identity, and what did she just switch on?

## 7. Cliffhanger patterns

| Pattern | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Costly choice | Two real losses; the lead must choose | Nia can stop the elevator only by giving up her mother's keycard, which the machine keeps. Both costs were shown earlier |
| Goal-changing reveal | The objective rested on false information | Nia reaches the office she expected to be her mother's. The nameplate says Nia |
| Threat crosses a boundary | The danger enters something that felt safe | Nia calls her brother and the elevator's voice answers from his phone |
| Control reversal | A small win completes someone else's plan | She hits the emergency button; a text arrives: "Thank you for confirming you are alone" |
| Irreversible action | A line crossed, cannot be undone | She steps out, the doors close, the display reads OCCUPIED |
| Reinterpreted detail | A planted detail takes a new meaning | A scratch on the keycard in the opening is now on the back of her hand |

Irreversible actions to reach for: sending a message, signing, opening a door, revealing an identity, choosing one person over another, destroying evidence, entering or leaving a protected place.

## 8. Weak and strong endings

| Weak | Why it fails | Stronger |
|---|---|---|
| "Someone is coming." | No who, why or change | The intercom uses the private nickname her mother used earlier |
| A masked stranger appears | No tie to the episode | Footage shows her own keycard opened the corridor door |
| "To be continued" | Names the format, asks nothing | The floor she escaped is now the destination on her phone map |
| A loud sound | Sound alone changes nothing | The alarm starts after her keycard confirms her identity |
| Cut before an answer | The story merely paused | She answers the call and hears her own conversation from ten seconds ago |

The stronger version uses an object, rule, relationship, choice or action already in the episode. The surprise is a reinterpretation, not a delivery from outside.

## 9. Build hook and ending as one chain

Do not write the first and last seconds as separate tricks. The hook plants what the ending changes:

**Hook evidence, character response, escalation, reinterpretation, consequence.**

For the elevator: the elevator reaches a floor that does not exist; Nia presses the emergency control and studies her mother's keycard; the doors open on an unfinished corridor and the lobby button is dead; a photo downloads showing Nia in the elevator with a figure behind her; the intercom says, "Welcome back, Nia. Present your keycard."

Plan one **double-duty detail**: first it reads as ordinary information, then, after the turn, as evidence. The keycard, a scratch, a phrase, a ring. Show it clearly in the first ten seconds so it pays off at the end.

**Escalation is not repetition.** Between hook and ending, every beat must make things worse, tighter, or more costly. Remove an easy exit, add a witness, raise the price, shorten the clock, or turn the evidence against the lead. A middle that repeats the same humiliation or the same argument is flat even if each beat is well written.

Across a series, vary the ending function (recognition, costly choice, relationship reversal, goal reversal, irreversible action) while keeping the same central conflict.

## 10. When a smaller payoff is right

Not every episode needs a threat or a last-second reveal. Constant shocks make episodes interchangeable. A quieter ending is right when the main question can be answered emotionally, when the lead makes a choice and has to live with it, or when the next episode should open on consequences. Deleting a message, returning an object, refusing an offer or choosing not to tell all move the story. Even then, the ending must change something the viewer can see, and the viewer must know what to watch for next.

## 11. The episode brief

Fill this in before writing shots. Keep it with the episode.

```
Protagonist / immediate intention / location:

HOOK
  Visible abnormal detail:
  Viewer question:
  Why the lead cannot ignore it:
  Cold-viewer line (who, relation, wrong, next move):

ESCALATION
  Action the lead takes:
  New pressure or evidence:
  Easy option that disappears:

TURN
  Earlier detail that changes meaning (the double-duty detail):
  What the lead now understands:

ENDING
  Decision, reveal, threat or consequence:
  Why it grows from this episode:
  Exact next-episode question:
  Final image:

Recurring prop / visual detail:
Continuity requirements (characters, locations, wardrobe states):
```

If the ending cannot be tied to the opening evidence, revise the chain before storyboards or video.

## 12. Checks before returning

- Does second 1 to 3 show a person in a problem, not scenery or setup?
- Does the cold-viewer line fill in from the opening alone, with sound off?
- Is context carried by objects, positions or captions rather than narration?
- Does something get worse at least every 10 to 20 seconds, and does anything repeat?
- Is a double-duty detail planted early and used at the end?
- Does the ending change the situation and pose one specific question?
- If the ending is a smaller payoff, is that a choice and not a gap?
