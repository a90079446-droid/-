## 1. Write what the camera can see

An action line is an instruction for an image. If it cannot be filmed or generated, it is a note, not an action.

| Unfilmable | Filmable |
|---|---|
| She suddenly realizes her husband has been lying for years. | She finds a second wedding ring in his jacket pocket. Her smile disappears. |
| He feels guilty. | He can't look at the photo on the desk. He turns it face down. |
| They have a complicated history. | She still knows how he takes his coffee. She makes it without asking. He notices. |
| The room is tense. | Nobody touches the food. The clock is loud. |
| She remembers the accident. | Rain on the windshield. Her hands tighten on the wheel at the same intersection. |

The inner life still matters. Keep motivation in the story outline and character notes; in the screenplay, express it through what characters **do, choose, avoid, and say indirectly**.

## 2. Translating inner states into behaviour

A method:
1. Name the inner state precisely (not "sad": "humiliated in front of her son").
2. Ask what this character would *do* about it in the next five seconds, given their personality.
3. Choose the most specific, small, readable action.
4. Pair it with an object or a person when possible; interaction reads better than solo emoting.

Examples:
- Humiliated pride: she picks up the coins he threw, one by one, without hurrying, and puts them in the tip jar.
- Suppressed jealousy: he laughs at the rival's joke a second too late.
- Love that cannot be spoken: she re-ties his scarf, and leaves her hands there a moment.
- Decision: she deletes the draft message. Then types one word. Sends it.

## 3. Faces, bodies, eyes

- **Faces:** micro-changes read in close-up: a smile that stops, a swallow, eyes going wet but no tears, a tiny nod.
- **Eye contact:** who looks at whom, who looks away first, a look held too long. Eyelines tell the audience where the power is.
- **Body language:** turning toward or away; open or closed posture; physical distance; who stands, who sits; who moves, who stays still.
- **Physical reactions:** a step back, a hand to the table to steady, setting something down too carefully.
- Vary the repertoire. Avoid every character "clenching fists", "jaw tightening", "tears streaming".

## 4. Positioning and blocking

- **Scene geography:** establish where people are relative to each other and to key objects (door, table, window). Keep it consistent (see `scene-construction.md`).
- **Status in space:** standing over someone, blocking a doorway, sitting at the head of the table, physically turning a back.
- **Movement as a beat:** a character crossing the room is a decision; write it when it matters.
- **Distance as relationship:** characters move closer or farther as the scene's emotional line moves.
- In vertical frames, favour vertical relationships (stairs, standing/seated) and pairs over wide groups.

## 5. Props as storytellers

- A good prop carries meaning: a ring, a key, a phone screen, a letter, a medicine bottle, an unworn uniform, a child's drawing.
- Introduce it visually before it matters (plant), use it when it matters (payoff).
- Use props to externalize choice: she keeps the key; she leaves it on the table.
- Track props: who has them, which hand, where they are put down (see `shot-continuity.md`).
- One important prop per moment is easier to read, and to generate, than several.

## 6. Environment and symbolism

- Settings express situation: a cramped kitchen versus a cold, huge dining room; rain on the night of the betrayal is a cliché unless used with intent.
- Light and time of day can carry mood; keep them consistent within a scene.
- Visual motifs across episodes (an unopened gift, a cracked mirror, a red umbrella) build meaning, but only if they recur and develop.
- Symbolism should work on the literal level first. If the audience misses the symbol, the scene must still make sense.

## 7. Camera-relevant writing (only when asked)

In a standard screenplay, imply the shot through the writing: "Her hand, on the doorknob. It doesn't turn." already reads as a close-up. Add explicit camera language (CU, OTS, POV, dolly, handheld) **only** in production mode or when the user asks for shots. Then see `shot-continuity.md` and `ai-video-production.md`.

## 8. Visual red flags

- Action lines describe thoughts, backstory, or feelings with no visible expression.
- Important information is only in dialogue when it could be seen.
- Key moments depend on tiny details that won't read on a phone screen.
- Multiple simultaneous actions in one moment, so the eye doesn't know where to look.
- Characters' positions are unclear or change without movement.
- Repetitive gestures across characters.
