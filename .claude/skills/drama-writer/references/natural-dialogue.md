## 1. The test

For every line, ask:
1. Would this person say it, with their background, mood, and relationship to the listener?
2. Would they say it *here* (public or private, rushed or idle, whispered or shouted)?
3. Would they say it this *completely*? People under emotion rarely speak in complete, balanced sentences.
4. Does the listener already know this? If yes, why are they being told?
5. Does the line do something: attack, defend, deflect, seduce, test, hide, confess, stall?

Dialogue is action. Each line is a move one character makes on another.

## 2. Tools of spoken language

Use these where they fit the person and moment, never all at once:

- **Contractions** in languages that have them. "I'm not going" not "I am not going", unless the formality is the point (emphasis, anger, a non-native speaker, a very formal character).
- **Fragments.** "Since when?" "Your mother. Again." "Not tonight."
- **Interruptions.** Mark with `--` at the end of the cut-off line: `DANI: I only told her because--` / `RISA: Because you wanted her to know.`
- **Trailing off.** An ellipsis when the speaker abandons the thought: `I thought maybe...`
- **Hesitation** words in the character's language ("um", "I mean", "well"; "eh", "anu"; "那个"; "저기"; "えっと"; "este", "o sea") sparingly, and only for characters who hesitate.
- **Short answers.** "Fine." "Okay." "No." Often more loaded than a paragraph.
- **Answering a different question.** "Where were you?" / "Did you eat?"
- **Meaningful repetition.** Repeating the other person's word back as a challenge: "Busy?" Or a character repeating a phrase because they cannot get past it.
- **Sarcasm and understatement**, when the character uses them.
- **Casual expressions and slang** appropriate to age, region, class, and era, in moderation. Slang dates quickly and can feel like a costume.
- **Overlapping or parallel talk** in group scenes (two people talking past each other).

## 3. Subtext

The best dialogue scenes are about something other than what the words say.

**Techniques**
- **Deflection:** changing the subject to avoid the real one. "Is she pretty?" / "The soup's getting cold."
- **Indirect question:** asking about something adjacent. Instead of "Are you leaving me?": "Did you take the blue suitcase?"
- **Contradictory behaviour:** words say one thing, actions another ("I'm fine," she says, folding the same shirt for the third time).
- **Politeness as a weapon:** excessive courtesy to someone you hate.
- **Avoidance:** a character never says a certain name or word, and the audience notices.
- **Uncomfortable silence:** let a question hang; write the silence as action (`He doesn't answer. He keeps peeling the orange.`).
- **Hidden intentions:** the character wants something the other must not know, so every line serves two purposes.
- **Saying the opposite:** "Go, then. I'll be fine." (She won't.)

**The audience should often understand more than the characters say.** Plant the real issue visually or in an earlier scene, then let the dialogue circle it.

**When to drop subtext.** At the climax of a long-suppressed conflict, a plain, direct line can hit like a hammer, *because* everything before it was indirect. "You were never coming back for me." Use direct emotional statements rarely and at the right moment.

## 4. Exposition without speeches

Never "As you know, Bob..." (characters telling each other what both know).

Ways to deliver needed information:
- **Conflict over the fact:** two characters disagree about it, so both state it from their side.
- **A character who genuinely doesn't know:** a newcomer, a child, a stranger, a police officer, but only if they have a reason to ask.
- **A visual document:** a letter, a contract, a photo, a hospital wristband, a message preview. Keep on-screen text short and legible.
- **Implication:** "Third time this month" tells us about a pattern without a history lesson.
- **Slip of the tongue:** a character reveals something they shouldn't, and the other notices.
- **Delay:** the audience can wait. Not every fact needs to arrive in episode 1.

## 5. Scenes with three or more speakers

Never assume conversation is two alternating speakers.

- **Give each person a distinct stake.** In a three-way confrontation, A wants a confession, B wants to protect C, C wants to leave. Lines come from these stakes.
- **Track who is addressing whom.** Use action lines or the content of the line (`DANI (to her mother):` or `[to Mother]` when needed for clarity), especially in scripts for voice generation or AI video where the target of the line affects staging.
- **Let alliances shift.** The person who was silent takes a side; the ally turns.
- **Not everyone speaks every turn.** A silent character reacting in frame is often the strongest presence.
- **Avoid round-robin.** A→B→C→A→B→C is mechanical. Real groups cluster: two people go back and forth, then a third cuts in.
- **Keep every speaker identifiable.** Exact same name every time. No "MOTHER" in one line and "MRS. LIM" in the next for the same person unless the script defines the change.
- **Reactions count.** Write the key reactions of non-speakers as action lines; they are part of the scene.

## 6. Dialogue length and runtime

In a 60–90-second episode, the whole script may have 120–220 spoken English words, and fewer if the episode leans on silence and action. Long speeches rarely survive.

- One line, one move. If a line has three moves, split it with a reaction or cut two of them.
- Long monologues are allowed only when the scene is built around them (a confession, a closing argument); even then, intercut with the listener's reaction.
- Estimate runtime properly (`runtime-timing.md`): speaking rate depends on language, emotion, and delivery; add time for pauses, reactions, and action.
- When cutting, remove repeated meaning first, then explanation, then politeness padding. Keep the line that hurts.

## 7. AI-dialogue tells to remove

These patterns mark dialogue as machine-written. Cut or rewrite them.

| Tell | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Naming the emotion | "I feel so betrayed and hurt right now." | Show it: "You let me cook for her." |
| Therapy-speak in ordinary people | "I need you to respect my boundaries and validate my feelings." | Only for characters who would really talk like this |
| Name in every line | "Sarah, I... Sarah, listen." | Use names rarely: to get attention, for emphasis, or as a weapon |
| Speechifying | A neat paragraph with a thesis and a moral | Fragments, interruption, a single sharp line |
| Over-complete grammar | "I did not expect that you would come here tonight." | "Didn't think you'd show." |
| Symmetrical exchange | Each line the same length and politeness | Vary length; let one person dominate |
| Poetic everyday talk | "The rain weeps for what we've lost." | Save lyricism for characters who are lyrical, at rare moments |
| Generic stock phrases | "We need to talk." "It's not what it looks like." "You don't understand!" "How dare you!" | Find the line only this character would say here |
| Explaining the subtext | "You're only saying that because you're jealous of my promotion." | Let the audience infer it |
| Everyone articulate | Everyone has the perfect comeback | People fumble, repeat, say the wrong thing |
| Artificial dramatic pauses | "I... I... I can't..." in every emotional line | One well-placed pause beats five |
| Overloaded slang | Every line stuffed with slang to sound "real" | Slang lightly, characteristic for this person |
| Reflexive questions as cliffhangers | "What do you mean...?" to end each scene | End on a move, not on confusion |

## 8. Revision passes

After a draft, do these passes in order:

1. **Voice pass:** cover the names; fix lines that could belong to anyone.
2. **Knowledge pass:** remove lines telling a character what they know; check nobody knows what they shouldn't.
3. **Subtext pass:** find the two most on-the-nose lines and make them indirect, unless they are the scene's deliberate direct strike.
4. **Compression pass:** trim each line to the shortest version that keeps the character's voice.
5. **Read-aloud pass:** say it (in your head, at speed). Anything you'd stumble on, an actor or TTS will too.
6. **Runtime pass:** check against the target (`runtime-timing.md`).
7. **Language pass:** register, honorifics, regional fit (`multilingual-writing.md`).
