Never apply one language's stylistic rules to another. "Use contractions" is English advice; "drop pronouns" is good advice for Korean, Japanese, Mandarin and Indonesian and bad advice for English.

## 1. Choosing the writing language

Priority:
1. Explicit instruction ("in Japanese", "English dialogue, Indonesian notes").
2. The established project language (series bible, earlier episodes).
3. The language of reference material, when it is meant as the model.
4. The language of the request.

The conversation language and the screenplay language can differ. Keep explanations in the user's language and the script in the writing language. If the instruction is ambiguous (Indonesian request, English-language reference, no instruction), choose by the priority above and state the choice in one short line so the user can switch.

Also settle the **variety**: American or British English; Mainland or Taiwan Mandarin; Spain or Mexican Spanish; Jakarta colloquial or standard Indonesian. If the audience is named ("for Filipino viewers", "for Taiwan"), let it decide. If not, choose the variety that fits the setting and characters, and keep it consistent.

## 2. Principles for every language

- **Write natively, not translated.** Draft directly in the target language. Do not draft in English and convert; it produces English word order, English idioms, and English emotional conventions in another language's words.
- **Register follows relationship and situation.** Every language has formal and informal speech; most dramas turn on shifts between them.
- **Address terms carry the relationship.** Many languages prefer titles, kinship terms or names over "you". Using the wrong one is a characterization choice, not a neutral default.
- **Regional speech is identity.** Use dialect or regional features for characters who would use them, and keep them consistent. Do not make a regional dialect the default voice of a whole cast, and don't use it as a joke unless the story intends that and handles it with respect.
- **Idioms are local.** Translate the *function* of an idiom, not its words.
- **Honorific and politeness systems are grammar.** Errors are as visible as spelling mistakes to native speakers.
- **Know your limits.** For lower-resource languages or specific dialects, write the best version you can, flag uncertain choices, and suggest native review.

## 3. Localization vs translation

When the user asks to adapt a story for another audience:
- Adapt **names** to the culture and to the characters' age and class.
- Adapt **settings** (a Jakarta kos, a Seoul officetel, a Taipei night market, a Madrid piso compartido).
- Adapt **institutions and situations** (exam systems, family obligations, workplace hierarchy, religious practice, holidays, food).
- Adapt **humour and idioms** to equivalents with the same function.
- Adapt **social dynamics**: what counts as a shocking breach (shouting at an elder, refusing a toast, divorce) varies by culture.
- Keep the **story engine**: the mechanism, stakes, and turns.
- Don't assume global audiences share one set of conventions. Check sensitive areas (religion, ethnicity, sexuality, politics, family authority) for the target market.

## 4. English

**Shared**
- Contractions are default in speech: "I'm", "don't", "you'd", "gonna/wanna" only for very casual speakers and in dialogue only.
- Under-statement and irony are common emotional strategies.
- Avoid stiff phrasings like "I am very upset with you", "Do not worry", or "Please calm yourself" unless the formality is a deliberate choice.

**American English (US)**: "apartment", "cell/phone", "check" (bill), "gotten", "mom", "vacation", "you guys", "y'all" (Southern US), "Sure", "No way", "Seriously?". Direct requests are normal among equals.

**British English (UK)**: "flat", "mobile", "bill", "mum", "holiday", "queue", "mate", "reckon", "brilliant", "proper" (as intensifier, informal), "fancy" (want), "cheers" (thanks). Tag questions ("You did, didn't you?") and understatement ("Bit of a problem.") carry a lot. Regional voices (Scottish, Northern English, London) differ markedly; don't blend them.

**Other varieties**: Australian, Irish, Indian, Singaporean/Malaysian, Nigerian, Filipino English each have their own vocabulary, rhythm and particles (e.g. Singlish "lah", "lor" in very informal speech). Use only when the setting calls for it and keep it light and consistent.

## 5. Indonesian

**Registers**
| Register | When | Markers |
|---|---|---|
| Baku (standard formal) | Speeches, news, official documents, very formal meetings | Full forms ("tidak", "sudah", "bagaimana", "saya", "Anda"), complete affixes |
| Standard conversational | Polite everyday talk, workplace, strangers, most cities | "saya/aku", "nggak/tidak", "sudah/udah" mixed; address by role (Bapak/Ibu, Mas/Mbak, Kak) |
| Jakarta colloquial | Friends, peers, young urban characters in Jakarta and media-influenced speech | "gue/gua", "lo/lu", "nggak/gak", "udah", "gimana", "banget", "kayak", "emang", "doang", "bokap/nyokap" |
| Regional | Characters from specific regions | Javanese-influenced: "Mas", "Mbak", "ndak", "to", "lho", "wis"; Sundanese-influenced: "Teh", "Aa/A'", "atuh", "mah"; Medan/Batak-influenced: "kau", "bah", "kek mana"; Eastern Indonesia: "beta" (I, Ambon), "ko"/"sa" (Papua) |

**Pronouns and address are relationship choices**
- "Gue–lo": peers in Jakarta (or characters who adopted it), casual, often edgy. Not for speaking to parents, elders, or superiors.
- "Aku–kamu": warm, personal, common between partners, friends outside Jakarta slang, and in many family settings.
- "Saya–Anda": formal, distant; "Anda" in spoken drama feels cold, bureaucratic, or deliberately distancing.
- Kinship and role terms replace "you": "Ibu mau ke mana?" (to one's mother or an older woman), "Mas udah makan?", "Kak, bantuin dong." Children address parents as "Ma/Mama/Bu/Ibu", "Pa/Papa/Pak/Ayah/Bapak" depending on family.
- Switching pronouns is drama. A couple who always used "aku–kamu" switching to "saya–kamu" in a fight signals cold distance. Moving from "gue–lo" to "aku–kamu" can mark romance getting serious.

**Discourse particles** (use with purpose; overuse sounds fake):
- "kok": surprise or mild challenge ("Kok kamu di sini?").
- "sih": softens or adds curiosity/emphasis in questions and complaints ("Kenapa sih?").
- "dong": insistence, coaxing ("Cerita dong.").
- "deh": persuasion, concession, resignation ("Ya udah deh.").
- "kan": appeal to shared knowledge ("Kan aku udah bilang.").
- "lho/loh": surprise, pointing something out ("Lho, kok gitu?").
- "nih/tuh": pointing to here/there, emphasis.
- "ya/yah": softening, seeking agreement; "yah" also disappointment.

**Rules**
- Do not force Jakarta slang onto every character. A grandmother in Yogyakarta, a Surabaya businessman, a Makassar fisherman, and a Jakarta teen speak differently.
- Formality level must match the relationship and stay consistent unless the change is meaningful.
- Pronoun dropping is natural ("Udah makan?" not "Apakah kamu sudah makan?").
- Avoid translation-ese: "Aku mencintaimu" is fine at a big moment but stiff in daily speech ("Aku sayang kamu" is more common in everyday intimacy); "Apa kamu baik-baik saja?" is a calque of "Are you okay?"; natural options include "Kamu nggak apa-apa?" or "Kamu kenapa?".
- English insertions ("literally", "which is", "jujur") are realistic for some urban young characters; a feature of their voice, not everyone's.

## 6. Mandarin Chinese

**Varieties and script**
- **Mainland (Putonghua)**: Simplified characters. Vocabulary e.g. 视频, 出租车, 软件, 质量, 打车, 手机. Northern speech may use 儿化 (哪儿, 一会儿) and words like 咋, 啥. Quotation marks “ ”.
- **Taiwan (Guoyu)**: Traditional characters. Vocabulary e.g. 影片, 計程車, 軟體, 品質, 機車 (scooter; also "annoying" as slang). Sentence-final 啦, 喔, 耶, 齁 more common; softer intonation patterns. Quotation marks 「 」.
- **Others**: Singapore/Malaysia Mandarin, Hong Kong (where characters may switch to Cantonese; written Cantonese is a different register). Ask or follow the setting.

**Natural speech**
- Pronoun dropping: Chinese omits subjects and objects that are clear from context. "吃了吗？" not "你吃了饭了吗？" in casual speech.
- Sentence-final particles carry tone: 吧 (suggestion, assumption), 呢 (follow-up, softening), 啊/呀 (emotion, softening), 嘛 (obviousness, mild complaint), 吗 (yes/no question). Use as a native speaker would: frequently but not on every line.
- Address: 老公/老婆 (spouses), 爸/妈, 哥/姐 (siblings, also friendly address), 阿姨/叔叔 (older adults), 师傅 (workers, drivers; Mainland), 老板, job titles (王总, 李经理, 张老师). Names: full name for anger or formality ("林晓！"), given name or nickname (小林, 晓晓, 阿明) for closeness.
- 您 shows respect, especially in northern Mainland speech; overuse sounds servile.
- Four-character idioms (成语) suit educated or formal characters in moderation; heavy use sounds written.
- Short-drama genre language exists (霸总 domineering CEO, 逆袭 comeback, 打脸 face-slap payoff). Know the tropes; do not write as if every story must use them.

**Translation-ese to avoid**
- Over-explicit pronouns (我, 你, 他 in every clause).
- 被 passives where active is natural.
- "我很抱歉" for every apology: 对不起, 不好意思, 抱歉 differ in weight and situation.
- "亲爱的" in everyday couple talk where real couples would use names, nicknames, or nothing.
- "你还好吗？" as a reflexive calque of "Are you okay?"; in many contexts 你没事吧？ or 怎么了？ is more natural.
- Long English-style relative clauses (那个昨天在公司门口和你说话的穿红衣服的男人) in speech.

## 7. Korean

**Speech levels** (choose per speaker–listener pair and keep consistent):
| Level | Endings | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 합쇼체 (formal deferential) | -습니다/-ㅂ니다, -습니까 | Announcements, military, formal business, addressing a group, very formal moments |
| 해요체 (polite) | -아요/-어요 | Default polite speech to strangers, colleagues, older people in casual settings |
| 해체 반말 (casual) | -아/-어, -지, -야 | Close friends, younger people, siblings, partners |
| 해라체 (plain) | -다, -냐/-니, -자, -라 | Writing, diaries, adults to children, older to much younger, some rough or commanding speech |

- The honorific infix **-시-** shows respect to the subject (어머니 오셨어요?). Humble forms (드리다, 여쭤보다, 뵙다) when the speaker does something for a superior.
- **Speech-level shifts are plot events.** "말 놓을게요" / "말 편하게 해" (permission to switch to 반말) marks growing closeness. Sudden 반말 to a superior in a fight is an insult or a break. A couple who normally use 반말 switching to 존댓말 signals coldness or sarcasm.

**Address**
- Avoid "당신" as a generic "you": in conversation it is used between spouses, in confrontation (rude), or in songs and writing. "너" is 반말.
- Prefer titles, kinship terms and names: 오빠/형 (older brother or close older male, by a female/male speaker), 언니/누나 (older female, by female/male speaker), 선배, 팀장님, 사장님, 선생님, 어머님/아버님 (in-laws), 이모 (friendly to older women, e.g. restaurant staff).
- "이름 + 씨" for peers/subordinates in polite contexts, not for superiors. Names: given name + -아/-야 (지우야, 민준아) for close friends and family.
- Pronouns are mostly dropped.

**Natural speech**: 진짜?, 뭐야, 됐어, 어떡해, 그니까/그러니까, 잠깐만, 아니 (as a discourse starter), 왜 (as "why" or as a protest). Interjections 아, 어, 헐 (young, casual). Avoid overly literal renderings of English emotional statements ("나는 너에게 매우 실망했어" is stiff; "너한테 진짜 실망이다" or simply "...실망이야" sounds natural).

## 8. Japanese

**Politeness**
- 丁寧語 (です/ます) default polite speech; plain form (だ, -る) among friends and family.
- 尊敬語 (respectful: いらっしゃる, おっしゃる, 召し上がる) for superiors' actions; 謙譲語 (humble: 参る, 申す, いたす) for one's own actions toward superiors. Service and business scenes rely on these.
- Switching from polite to plain is intimacy or aggression; switching from plain to polite is distance, sarcasm, or anger held down.

**Address and pronouns**
- Names with suffixes: さん (default), くん (younger males, juniors), ちゃん (children, close friends, cute), 様 (customers, very formal), 先輩/先生/部長 (titles).
- "あなた" is uncommon in direct address; it can sound distant or, for older couples, wifely. Use names and titles; drop pronouns.
- First-person pronouns encode character: 私 (neutral/polite), 僕 (softer male), 俺 (rough/casual male), あたし (casual female), うち (some female speakers, Kansai).
- Gendered sentence endings (わ, ぜ, ぞ, かしら) are more stylized than real modern speech; use for character, not as a default female/male setting.

**Natural speech**: sentence-final ね (agreement), よ (assertion), よね, かな, じゃん (casual, Kanto); aizuchi (うん, そう, へえ, まじで). Kansai-ben (や, あかん, ほんま, なんでやねん, おおきに) for Kansai characters, consistently. Japanese often avoids direct refusal and confrontation; the unsaid carries weight, which is a gift for subtext.

**Formatting**: 「」 for quotes, 『』 for quotes within quotes, 、 。 … (two characters: ……), ー for long vowels.

## 9. Spanish

- **Address**: tú (informal), usted (formal or respectful; in some regions also used between family members), vos (voseo: Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, much of Central America, parts of Colombia: "vos sabés", "vení"). Plural: vosotros (Spain informal) vs ustedes (Latin America, all plural "you").
- **Vocabulary varies**: coche (ES) / carro (MX, Caribbean, Colombia) / auto (AR, CL); móvil (ES) / celular (LatAm); ordenador (ES) / computadora (LatAm); vale (ES); órale, ¿mande? (MX); che (AR). Swearing and slang are highly regional and change meaning across borders; check before using.
- **Natural speech**: diminutives for affection or softening (ahorita, cafecito); discourse markers (pues, o sea, bueno, mira, oye, ¿eh?); subject pronouns usually dropped ("¿Vienes?" not "¿Tú vienes?" unless contrastive).
- **Punctuation**: opening ¿ and ¡ are required. Spanish prose opens dialogue with the raya, a long dash; in screenplay format, use the template's NAME: line format instead.

## 10. Other languages: a working protocol

For any other language (Thai, Vietnamese, Tagalog, Hindi, Arabic, Portuguese, French, German, Turkish, Malay, Russian, and so on):
1. Identify the **variety** (e.g. Brazilian vs European Portuguese; Egyptian vs Gulf vs Levantine Arabic; Hindi vs Hinglish; Malaysian vs Indonesian Malay, which are not interchangeable).
2. Identify **formality systems** (T–V distinctions like tu/vous, du/Sie, ты/вы; honorific particles such as Thai ครับ/ค่ะ; Tagalog po/opo).
3. Identify **address conventions** (kinship terms, titles, names).
4. Decide where **spoken vs written** forms diverge (Arabic: dialect in speech, Modern Standard Arabic in writing and news; decide what the characters speak and keep it consistent).
5. Write natively; avoid calqued idioms and English word order.
6. Flag any part where you are less confident (a regional dialect, slang, a cultural practice) in a short note after the script.

## 11. Code-switching

Real bilingual speakers mix languages: Indonesian–English in urban Jakarta, Taglish in Manila, Hinglish, Spanglish, Singlish, Korean with English loanwords, Hong Kong Cantonese with English terms.
- Use it only for characters who would, and in situations where they would (more with peers, less with grandparents).
- Keep the mix believable: switching happens at natural points (nouns, set phrases, emotional bursts), not randomly mid-word.
- In TTS production, mixed-language lines may need provider support for multilingual voices; note it.

## 12. Punctuation and script formatting

- Use each language's punctuation inside dialogue: full-width punctuation for Chinese and Japanese (，。？！); Spanish ¿ ¡; French spacing before ? ! : ; in French typography; Korean uses Western punctuation.
- Ellipses: "..." or "…" in Latin scripts; "……" in Chinese and Japanese.
- Keep character names identical throughout: same script, same spelling, same romanization.
- Scene headings may stay in English (INT./EXT.) for production pipelines, or be localized (内景/外景, 실내/실외) if the user's team prefers. Ask or follow project convention.

## 13. Multilingual review

Before returning:
- Is the requested language and variety used throughout, including for minor characters?
- Does any line read like a translation from English (word order, idioms, explicit pronouns, emotional phrasing)?
- Are speech levels, honorifics and pronouns consistent per relationship, and do shifts happen only for a reason?
- Are regional features used for the right characters and kept consistent?
- Is punctuation correct for the language?
- Are there any parts where a native-speaker review is advisable? Say so briefly.
