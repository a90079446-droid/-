## 1. What a scene is

A scene is a unit of continuous time and place in which at least one character pursues a goal against resistance, and something changes by the end. If nothing changes, it is not a scene yet; it may be a shot, a transition, or a cut.

Three continuity layers, kept distinct:
- **Story continuity:** the timeline, who knows what, relationships, rules (see `episode-continuity.md`).
- **Scene continuity:** the physical world inside one scene: layout, positions, light, weather, props, clothes, states.
- **Shot continuity:** how consecutive shots connect: progression of actions across cuts, eyelines, hand positions (see `shot-continuity.md`).

## 2. Building a scene

Before writing, answer:
1. **Whose scene is it?** The character whose want drives it.
2. **What do they want here, concretely?** ("Get him to sign tonight.")
3. **What resists?** (He wants to wait until his mother arrives.)
4. **What tactics will each side use?** Charm, guilt, threat, bribe, silence, truth.
5. **What is the turn?** The moment the scene changes direction.
6. **What is different at the end?** Knowledge, power, relationship, emotional state.
7. **What must the audience see?** Key visual information and props.

Then outline the scene in beats: each beat is one exchange of action and reaction, one tactic tried.

## 3. Entering late, leaving early

- Start after the greetings, after the arrival, at the point where the conflict is live.
- End as soon as the turn has landed and its consequence is clear. Don't let characters summarize the scene to each other.
- In short-form, it's common to start mid-sentence or mid-action ("--and you didn't think I'd find out?").

## 4. The turn

Every important scene has a moment where it flips:
- New information arrives (a document, a confession, a phone call, an arrival).
- A tactic fails and a new one begins (charm fails; she threatens).
- A character makes a decision.
- Power shifts from one person to another.

Place the turn late enough to build pressure, early enough to show its consequence. In a 60–90-second episode, the turn often lands around two-thirds of the way through.

## 5. Scene geography and staging

At the start of each scene, establish (in the heading, a short action line, or both):
- **Location** and **time of day** (and weather if visible or relevant).
- **Who is present** and roughly **where** (by the window, at the table, in the doorway).
- **Key objects** that will matter (the envelope on the counter).

Then keep it consistent. If she's by the window, she must cross to reach the door. If the envelope is on the counter, it stays there until someone moves it.

Simple geography helps everyone: actors, directors, and AI video generators. Avoid layouts that need a diagram.

## 6. Scene continuity

Maintain inside a scene:
- **Room layout:** doors, windows, furniture; which side is which.
- **Character positions:** standing/sitting, where, facing whom.
- **Lighting and time of day:** a scene that starts at dusk does not end at noon without a cut that implies time passing.
- **Weather:** if it's raining outside the window, it's raining throughout.
- **Props:** location and state (open, closed, full, empty, broken).
- **Clothing and appearance:** jacket on/off, wet hair, a cut on the hand, makeup running.
- **Physical states:** injured, drunk, out of breath; these persist and fade gradually.
- **Ongoing actions:** if he was chopping vegetables, the board is still there, the knife somewhere specific.

Never reset a character's position or an object's location without showing the move. If the story needs the change, write the action that causes it.

## 7. Transitions between scenes

- A cut between scenes should be motivated: a time jump, a location change, a parallel action, a consequence.
- Make time jumps legible: light change, clothes change, a line ("Three days, and she still hasn't called."), a visual marker.
- **Transitional devices with meaning:** a match cut (the ring dropped in one scene, a ring placed on a pillow in the next), a sound bridge (a phone ringing that is answered in the next scene), a contrast cut (laughter at a party → silence in a hospital corridor).
- Avoid establishing shots that only show a building, unless the building tells us something. In short-form, often cut straight into the action.

## 8. Scene red flags

- No one wants anything specific.
- The scene ends where it began.
- Two scenes in a row do the same job.
- Characters arrive and leave with no reason.
- Positions and props jump around.
- The scene exists only to deliver information that could be delivered faster elsewhere.
