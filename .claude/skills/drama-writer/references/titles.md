A good title is one sentence's worth of story. A stranger who reads only the title should be able to say who it is about, what happened to them, and what the turn is. A title that sounds impressive but tells you nothing fails, however polished.

## 1. Two titles, two jobs

A concept needs two kinds of title. Do not blend them into one.

| | Series title | Upload title |
|---|---|---|
| Where it lives | Catalog, playlist, project folder, series bible | Video caption or headline for one episode or clip |
| Length | Short: about two to five words, or a short phrase | One breath, long enough to carry a whole turn |
| Job | Name the series and promise its core pleasure | Make a stranger stop and want the answer |
| Shape | A role plus a predicament, or a single striking object | Trigger, consequence, reveal |
| Punctuation | Plain | Often "!" or "?", sometimes a hashtag or emoji (see section 7) |

Series-title shapes seen in short-drama catalogs, shown here with original examples:
- **Role plus predicament:** "The Wrong Bride Wins", "Istri Kedua Tak Mau Mengalah".
- **Status flip in a phrase:** "Grumpy CEO, Now Her Biggest Fan", "Bos Galak Jadi Bucin".
- **One odd object that carries the story:** "The Borrowed Umbrella", "Payung Pinjaman".
- **A short event:** "Dumped at the Airport", "Diusir di Hari Ulang Tahun".

Upload titles can run long. They still have to be one readable sentence or two short ones, not a paragraph.

## 2. The plain-sentence test

Before offering a title, write the story in one plain sentence from the six premise answers (`references/storytelling.md` §1). Then check the title against it:

1. **Who:** can a stranger tell who the title is about (a role, a relationship or a name, not "someone")?
2. **What happened:** does it name an event or a situation, not a mood?
3. **The turn:** does it show the reversal, the secret or the mismatch that makes it a story?
4. **True:** will the episode or series deliver exactly what the title promises?

If a title fails 1 to 3, it is decoration. If it fails 4, it is clickbait, and the viewer who feels cheated will not come back. Every claim in a title must be something the story does.

Mood and abstraction titles ("Echoes of the Heart", "Takdir Cinta", "A Love Beyond Time") name a feeling and no situation. Do not offer them unless the user asks for that style.

## 3. Series titles

1. Lead with the role or the predicament, in the words the audience would use to describe the show to a friend.
2. Keep it short enough to read as a catalog tile.
3. Promise the core pleasure (`references/storytelling.md` §6): a title for a revenge drama should signal being wronged or getting even, a title for a romance should signal the pairing or the obstacle.
4. Do not use a genre label as a title ("A CEO Romance"). That is a category, not a situation.
5. Offer one recommended series title and, when useful, one or two alternatives that differ in angle (the predicament, the role, the object), not three rewordings of the same title.

## 4. Upload titles

Build an upload title from three parts, in whatever order reads best in the language:

- **Trigger:** the thing that happens or goes wrong, concretely.
- **Consequence:** what it sets off.
- **Reveal or contradiction:** the turn the viewer wants to see resolved.

Example, in Indonesian: "Salah Masuk Lift, Dia Malah Berdua dengan Bos yang Phobia Perempuan!" (trigger: wrong lift; consequence: alone with the boss; contradiction: the boss avoids women.)

Example, in English: "She Booked the Wrong Table on a Blind Date. The Man Across From Her Is Her New Boss."

Rules:
- Name a specific thing: a table, a lift, a pair of shoes, a fried-rice recipe. Specifics are what make a title feel real.
- Use one reveal, not three. A title that lists every twist has nothing left for the episode.
- Put the strongest word where a truncated line will still show it. Titles are often cut off in feeds, so the hook should come early.
- Write a different upload title for each episode or clip from that episode's own turn. Do not reuse the series premise as every episode's caption.
- Do not add a question or exclamation mark by reflex. Use "!" or "?" when the line is a real exclamation or a real question.

## 5. Patterns that work

These recur in real short-drama titles. Use them as shapes to fill with the story's own facts, not as a menu to pick from at random. All examples below are original.

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Misbelief, then reveal | "I thought X... turns out Y" | "Kukira Dia Cuma Kurir, Ternyata Pemilik Gedung Ini!" |
| Mishap, then jackpot | A small mistake lands the lead somewhere powerful | "Salah Ambil Kopi, Dia Malah Diterima Kerja oleh Direktur Galak!" |
| Betrayal, then upgrade, with a secret | The lead is wronged, rises, and carries something hidden | "Ditinggal Tunangan Sebelum Akad! Pria yang Menolongnya Ternyata Ayah dari Bayinya" |
| Contradiction | A person acts against their stated rule | "The CEO Who Hates Touch Won't Let Go of Her Hand" |
| Odd-object hook | One concrete, unexpected object or animal carries the plot | "Kucing-Kucing Itu Merekam Semua Rahasia Rumah Ini" |
| Status flip | The humiliated one is revealed to hold the power | "Dihina Karena Jadi Tukang Cuci Piring, Ternyata Pemilik Restorannya" |

A pattern earns its place only if the story really has that mechanism. Do not force a misbelief-reveal title onto a story with no misbelief.

## 6. Spoilers: what a title may give away

`references/storytelling.md` §8 warns against a logline that makes the ending obvious. A title is allowed to be more forward, because its job is to start the viewer's question, not to hide the story.

- **A title may give away** the mechanism and the first reveal: who the boss really is, what the lead has been hiding, which choice started it.
- **A title should not give away** the final outcome or the late-series twist: who wins in the end, who dies, the last betrayal.

Give the viewer the "what" and keep the "how" and "at what cost". If the title already answers the central dramatic question, rewrite it so the question stays open.

## 7. Language and platform

- **Write the title natively in the writing language.** Do not translate a title from another language. Phrases such as "istri sah", "bucin", "ternyata", "CEO galak" or "kontrak nikah" belong to specific markets and carry connotations a literal translation loses. See `references/multilingual-writing.md`.
- **Follow the audience's vocabulary** for the roles in the title (CEO, direktur, bos, presdir, 总裁, 社長), as real viewers would say them.
- **Emoji and hashtags are platform choices.** Leave them off by default. Add them when the user names a platform or style that uses them, and keep to a small number. Do not invent platform rules, length limits or ranking effects. If the user needs a hard limit, ask them to confirm it for their platform.
- **Mixed languages:** match how the target audience writes titles. If local titles mix in English words, do the same. If they do not, do not.
- **For languages or markets you cannot write natively,** say so, give your best version, and suggest native review.

## 8. Delivering a title pack

When asked for titles, or when a concept needs a title, deliver:

- **Series title:** one recommendation, plus one or two alternatives if the user wants options.
- **Upload titles:** three to five, each from a different pattern or angle, for the episode or the series opener.
- **Pick:** one line saying which upload title you would use and why, in terms of the plain-sentence test.
- **Notes:** only if needed, such as a spoiler decision or a language risk.

Keep it scannable. If the user asked only for a series title or only for upload titles, deliver only that. If the user gave sample titles they like, match their register and length, and borrow their shape, not their words.

## 9. Title red flags

- It names a mood or theme and no situation.
- A stranger could not say who it is about.
- It promises something the story does not deliver.
- It gives away the final outcome.
- It states the twist, the cost and the ending in one title.
- Every title in the pack uses the same pattern.
- It is a genre label ("A CEO Romance") or a stock phrase that fits any story.
- It borrows a real series' title, characters or distinctive wording.
- It stacks emoji and hashtags the platform or the user did not ask for.
- The translation reads like another language's title in this language's words.
