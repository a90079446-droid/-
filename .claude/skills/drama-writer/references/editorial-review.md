# Editorial review and self-review

Two uses:
- **Self-review** before returning any screenplay or story: run silently and fix what fails. Don't print this checklist unless the user asks.
- **Editorial review (Workflow E)** of the user's material: give a diagnosis and corrections.

## 1. Story review
- Is the premise interesting and specific, with a clear mechanism?
- Is the central conflict understandable within the first seconds?
- Does the hook work, and does the story deliver on it?
- Does the hook pass the scroll, sound-off and specificity tests (`storytelling.md` §5)? Which engine does it run on: curiosity, anticipation, desire?
- Can you finish the line "By now the viewer wants ___"? Does the episode pay part of it?
- Cold-viewer test: from the first 8 seconds with no sound, can a stranger say who, what relation, what wrong, what happens next (`hooks-and-cliffhangers.md` §3)? Is context shown, not narrated?
- Does each beat after the hook make things worse, tighter or costlier, or does one note repeat?
- Is a detail planted in the opening and reinterpreted at the ending? Does the ending change the situation and pose one specific next question?
- Does the story progress logically; does each beat cause the next?
- Are the stakes meaningful, personal, and visible?
- Are the characters' choices driving events (not coincidence)?
- Are twists planted, fair, and consequential?
- Does the ending pay off what was set up, with an appropriate cost?
- Any convenient coincidences, unmotivated betrayals, or idiot-plot moments?

## 2. Dialogue review
- Does each character sound distinct (cover-the-names test)?
- Does the dialogue sound spoken, not written?
- Is the register right for each speaker–listener pair?
- Are there lines explaining what both characters know?
- Is there subtext, or does everyone say exactly what they mean all the time?
- Are there AI tells: named emotions, therapy-speak, speeches, names in every line, stock phrases?
- Does the dialogue fit the runtime?
- Are tags and parentheticals useful and sparse?
- For TTS: are speaker names consistent, tags separable from spoken text, provider syntax correct?

**Voice pass (scripts that will be voiced).** Read each charged line aloud at speed.
- Does it have a surface and an underneath, and a single place where the underneath leaks?
- Does intensity move across the scene, and do the speakers contrast?
- Would the line sound the same said by a flat voice? If yes, put the performance in the words (restart, repetition, length, punctuation) before adding a tag.
- Is any reaction that matters audible?
- Does a delivery sheet cover the lines that carry the scene?

## 3. Character review
- Does each important character want something specific in each scene?
- Are behaviours consistent with established personality and history?
- Is any change motivated by on-screen events?
- Do characters know only what they should?
- Do relationships colour how people speak to each other?
- Are antagonists given reasons and competence?

## 4. Emotional credibility review
- Is each emotional peak caused by something the audience saw?
- Is intensity proportional to cause (or deliberately not, with the story aware of it)?
- Is there variety in emotional reactions?
- Do quiet moments exist where the audience needs them?
- Do emotional consequences carry over?

## 5. Pacing review
- Does every beat do a job (plot, character, tension, information, emotion, plant, payoff)?
- Is there repetition to cut?
- Is there a held beat after the key reveal?
- Does the episode start late and end early?
- Is the turn placed to allow its consequence to be felt?
- Is a new question open before the current one closes? Is there a re-hook where attention dips?
- Does the ending cut on the inhale, or hold until the tension is gone?

## 6. Production review
- Can every action be shown?
- Are scenes feasible to generate (characters per shot, actions per shot, hands, props, choreography)?
- Are locations, light, and time of day consistent within scenes?
- Are props tracked, with hands and states consistent?
- Did the adjacent-shot pass find repeated actions, teleporting props, posture jumps, unexplained time jumps, unmotivated cuts? Fixed?
- Are characters in plausible positions, with matching eyelines?
- Is on-screen text short or planned as an overlay?

## 7. Multilingual review
- Requested language and variety respected throughout?
- Native-sounding dialogue, no literal-translation artifacts?
- Regional expressions appropriate to the characters?
- Formality levels and honorifics consistent, with shifts only for reasons?
- Correct punctuation for the language?

## 8. Delivering an editorial review (Workflow E)

Structure:
1. **Overall verdict** in two or three sentences: what works, the biggest problem.
2. **Issues ranked by impact**, each with: where (scene/line), what's wrong, why it matters, and the fix. Group by area (story, character, dialogue, emotion, pacing, production, language) only if there are many.
3. **Revised version** of the material (or of the affected scenes, if the material is long), with the fixes applied.
4. **What changed**: a short list, so the user can accept or reject each change.

Rules:
- Corrections, not just criticism. Every issue comes with a concrete fix.
- Preserve the writer's intent, voice and style where it works; fix what doesn't.
- Don't invent problems to look thorough. If something works, say so briefly.
- Distinguish matters of craft (clear errors) from matters of taste (options).
- Be direct and specific. "Line 14: Rina tells Jun about the loan he negotiated; he already knows. Cut, or make her use it as an accusation: 'Twenty percent, Jun. You agreed to twenty percent.'"

## 9. Rewrite principles
- Fix the cause, not the symptom: a flat scene usually needs a sharper want or a better turn, not punchier lines.
- Make the smallest change that solves the problem, unless the user asked for a page-one rewrite.
- Keep the runtime in mind; a fix that adds 15 seconds needs a cut elsewhere.
- Re-run continuity checks after structural changes.
